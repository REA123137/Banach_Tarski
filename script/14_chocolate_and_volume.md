**RYM**  Thank you, Iasmina. *beat* — And I wish that worked.
But what we just proved was never about chocolate. It was about pieces with no volume at all: sets that are not measurable, in the sense of Lebesgue. Your bar, unfortunately, has a volume.
*beat* — Which means this one is rigged. And the rigging is rather pretty.

> **ANIMATION** — Before and after superimposed, then a tenfold magnification at the edge. The offset becomes visible.

**IASMINA**  Superimpose the before and the after: the rebuilt bar is very slightly shorter. Every row has lost a hair's width. Twenty-four hairs end to end make exactly one square.
We did not create chocolate. We spread it out. It is an optical illusion, and it can be measured.
*beat* — So why is the ball not the same swindle?
To answer, we need to say what a volume is. It is a way of attaching a number to a set of points, obeying two rules: the volumes of two disjoint pieces add up, and moving a piece does not change its volume.

> **ON SCREEN** — μ(X ⊔ Y) = μ(X) + μ(Y),  μ(ρ(X)) = μ(X)

**RYM**  That volume, the classical one, the one you will compute with integrals, is not defined on every set of points. It is defined on those you can approximate as closely as you like with stacks of little boxes. Those are the measurable sets.
A chocolate bar is measurable. A cube, a ball, some wildly irregular shape too. That is virtually everything you will ever meet.
*beat* — Our four batches are not.

> **ANIMATION** — Endless continuous zoom into P₁. At every scale, the same riddled texture. Never an edge, never a stable structure.

**IASMINA**  They are everywhere present and everywhere absent at once. In the tiniest ball imaginable, a batch has points, and is missing points. You can neither cover them with boxes from outside nor fill them from inside: the two approximations never meet, and no number imposes itself as the volume.
That is the whole difference. With chocolate, we cheat on the dimensions, and a ruler proves it. With the ball there is nothing to cheat on: the pieces have no volume at all.

**RYM**  And look at where they come from. The whole construction is explicit: the matrices are written down, the sorting is done on the first letter, anyone can redo it. Exactly one step was not: the choice of representatives.
It is the axiom of choice that manufactures these pieces, and nothing else.
So there is no contradiction. We did not break a rule: we exhibited objects the rule does not apply to.
And that is the real lesson. The theorem does not say you can manufacture matter. It says it is impossible to attach a volume to every subset of space. Not hard. *beat* — Impossible.

## Animation notes

**The scale that never settles.** Put a chocolate bar on a weighing scale: the needle lands cleanly. A cube: clean. A wildly irregular shape: clean.
Now put P₁ on it: the needle swings between two bounds, and the two never meet, however fine the boxes stacked underneath and above. Non-measurability, made mechanical. Let the needle swing through the entire explanation and never let it stop.
Leave the needle swinging under the closing exchange, and let it still be swinging when the picture cuts to black. It is the last thing the viewer sees move. — `S14ScaleNeverSettles`

The endless zoom is built from three uniform fields of dust a third of an octave apart, each doubling and recycling, weighted so the three weights always sum to the same number: the frame magnifies for ever at constant density, and membership of P₁ is re-drawn at every recycle. There is no magnification at which an edge appears, because there is no edge. — `S14EndlessZoom`
