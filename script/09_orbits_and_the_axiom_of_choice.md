# 09 · Orbits, representatives, and the axiom of choice

**11:15 – 14:05** · 410 words · Iasmina 205 / Rym 205
**Purpose** — define orbit and representative, say what they are for, then why choosing is impossible.

**Scenes** `S09Orbit · S09Representatives · S09ShoesSocks · S09AxiomCard · S09ImpossibleHand`

---

**RYM**  Write G for the set of all our rotations, that is, all our words turned into matrix products. And ρ will stand for any one element of G, one particular sequence of motions.

> **ON SCREEN** — G = ⟨A, B⟩,  ρ ∈ G

**RYM**  Now take a point of the sphere and apply to it every rotation in G, one after another. What you get is a set of points called its orbit.

> **ON SCREEN** — orbit of p = { ρ(p) : ρ ∈ G }

**RYM**  In plain English: the orbit of a point is the list of every place that point can be sent by our motions. Its complete itinerary, scattered across the sphere.

> **ANIMATION** — A single point. Its images appear one by one, faster and faster, until they form a dust scattered over the sphere.

**IASMINA**  Two points are in the same orbit if some rotation in G carries one to the other. Otherwise their orbits never meet.
So the sphere is divided into orbits, infinitely many, which never touch.
*beat* — In each orbit I now pick one point, exactly one, and call it that orbit's representative. And I write M for the set of all these representatives.

> **ANIMATION** — On the sphere, one point in each dust cloud lights up white. The set of white points is M.

**RYM**  Here is what M is for, and this is the important moment.
Take any point of the sphere. It belongs to one orbit, and only one. That orbit has a representative. And our point is obtained from that representative by applying one rotation, exactly one.

> **ON SCREEN** — every point p is p = ρ(m) for a unique m ∈ M and a unique ρ ∈ G

**IASMINA**  In other words, every point of the sphere finally carries a label: a representative, and a word. A starting address, and an itinerary.
And since we know how to sort words by their first letter, we now know how to sort points.
*slow down* — Except one question remains, and it is the deepest in the whole story. How do we pick those representatives?
Inside an orbit no point is special. There is no formula, no rule, no program that singles one out. And we must do it in infinitely many orbits, all at once.
Bertrand Russell explained this exact difficulty with shoes and socks.

> **ANIMATION** — Infinitely many shoeboxes: in every one, the left shoe lights up, all at once. Then infinitely many drawers of identical socks: nothing lights up.

**RYM**  Faced with infinitely many pairs of shoes, you say “I take the left one.” One sentence, and the choice is made everywhere at once, because there is a rule.
Faced with infinitely many pairs of identical socks, there is no left one. You know there is a sock in every drawer. You cannot say which.
Our orbits are socks.
So we accept an axiom, called the axiom of choice.

> **ON SCREEN** — For any family of non-empty sets, there is a function picking one element from each.

**IASMINA**  It asserts that the choice exists. It does not say how to make it. And that is not a failure of imagination on our part: it has since been proved that no recipe will ever exist.
We accept it, and M exists.
