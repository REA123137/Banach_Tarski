# 12 · The circle that closes up, and the poles

**18:35 – 20:55** · 334 words · Iasmina 194 / Rym 140
**Purpose** — prove the circle case, then use it for the poles and for the centre.

**Scenes** `S12Circle · S12Dartboard · S12Poles · S12Centre`

---

**IASMINA**  Let us start with a much simpler case, one dimension down. A circle with one point missing.

> **ANIMATION** — A clean circle, with one clearly visible black gap on its rim. We orbit around it, the gap stays.

**IASMINA**  It looks incomplete. It is not.

> **ON SCREEN** — A circle with one point removed is equidecomposable with the whole circle.

**IASMINA**  And the proof fits in one image. Call p the missing point, and take the rotation by one radian.
One radian is never a whole number of turns. So if I rotate p by one radian, then two, then three, I never land on the same place twice, and never on p itself.

> **ANIMATION** — From the gap, a ghost point departs and settles at one radian. Then another at two radians, another at three. They light up one by one, endlessly, never overlapping.

**RYM**  Those points form an infinite queue. Call it X.

> **ON SCREEN** — X = { the point p rotated by 1, 2, 3, … radians }

**RYM**  Now I cut my punctured circle into two pieces only: the queue X on one side, everything else on the other.
I leave the rest where it is, and I rotate the queue X by one radian backwards.

> **ANIMATION** — The key moment, played very slowly then looped three times. Every point of the queue steps back one place: the one at one radian fills the gap, the one at two radians takes the first one's place, and so on. The circle is whole. No point vanished, none was created.

**IASMINA**  Watch what happens. The first point of the queue fills the gap. The second takes the first one's place. The third takes the second's. And since the queue is infinite, there is never a last one leaving a hole behind it.
*beat* — Hold on to that picture, because we are about to use it twice.
First for the poles. Back to the sphere, and let us choose a new axis, different from every axis of our rotations, and a well-chosen angle.

**RYM**  Why can we always find one? Because the forbidden axes can be put in a list, one per rotation, whereas the axes of space cannot be listed. There are strictly more of them.

> **ANIMATION** — A dartboard riddled with isolated pinpricks, the forbidden axes. Throw a hundred darts at random: not one hits a prick.

**RYM**  Now rotate the poles about that new axis. Each pole traces a circle, and occupies a queue of positions on it.
So every orbit is exactly the situation we have just handled: a circle with one point missing.
We apply the same move. The whole queue steps back one place, the missing pole is filled in, and nobody is lost.

> **ON SCREEN** — The sphere with its poles removed is equidecomposable with the whole sphere.

**IASMINA**  And the centre of the ball? Exactly the same. Draw a circle inside the ball passing through the centre, and apply the circle theorem to it.

> **ON SCREEN** — The ball without its centre is equidecomposable with the whole ball.

**IASMINA**  So the points we set aside have come back. All of them.

## Animation notes

**The circle on a loop, then the whole sphere at once.** The closing circle must be the most carefully made animation in the video: very slow, looped three times, with a discreet counter showing that the queue never ends. — `S12Circle`; the queue travels *along the rim*, never across the chord.

Then a reveal: pull back, and discover that this circle was one of thousands, all drawn about the new axis, all punctured at the same instant, all closed by a single motion. One gesture becomes a global gesture. This is the scene that makes the proof feel solid. — `S12Poles`
